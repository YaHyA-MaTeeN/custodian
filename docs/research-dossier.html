AProduct & feature space16 items

The big discovery in this track: Google published exactly how Gmail's Priority Inbox works, and the design it describes is very close to what we independently arrived at. We should copy it rather than reinvent it.

A1–A3

Feature space, no-LLM features, and how tone is learned

Settled

Covered in the Field Manual §02 and §04. Summary: ten jobs ranked by value against effort; the three highest-value ones need no model at all; tone comes from retrieving the user's own past replies rather than asking them or fine-tuning.

A4

What is the right taxonomy of email types?

Strong evidence

This matters because tags, alerts and reminders all hang off it. If the taxonomy is wrong, everything downstream is wrong.

What the research says

The foundational work is Learning to Classify Email into Speech Acts (Cohen, Carvalho & Mitchell, EMNLP 2004). It treats every email as performing a speech act — a thing the sender is doing to you — and the useful categories are: request, propose, commit, deliver, amend. Later work on the Enron corpus narrows this further: the categories that actually matter for automation are request and propose, plus commit when it contains a date or time.

That's a genuinely important insight, and it's not the taxonomy most people would guess. The natural instinct is to classify by topic — work, personal, finance, shopping. But topic doesn't tell you what to do. Speech act does: a request needs a reply, a proposal needs a decision, a commitment needs a reminder.

Recommendation

Use two independent axes, not one list.

Axis 1 — what is being done to me (speech act): request · propose · commit · deliver · notify. This decides whether the user needs to act at all.

Axis 2 — what it's about (object): meeting · payment · document · deadline · account · social · bulk. This decides the lead time and the tone of the reminder.

Every downstream behaviour is a cell in that grid. Propose + meeting → draft an RSVP, remind 30 minutes before. Notify + payment → no reply needed, remind two days ahead and escalate. Two axes of five and seven is far easier to train a small model on than one flat list of thirty-five, and far easier to explain to a user.

A5

How do you capture someone's word choice, not just their register?

Settled

Three approaches exist: ask the user, fine-tune a per-user model, or retrieve their own past writing as examples. The style-transfer literature is clear that supervised fine-tuning does not beat in-context examples when style data is scarce — and for one person's email it is always scarce. It also finds that 2–5 examples suffice and imitation plateaus after 4–5; beyond that you pay tokens for nothing.

Newer hybrid work (per-author LoRA adapters, ASTRAPOP) does beat pure in-context learning, but needs training infrastructure per user. That is a V3+ optimization, not a V2 mechanism.

Recommendation

Retrieve exactly 4–5 exemplars from the user's Sent folder, chosen by embedding similarity to the incoming message. No training, no GPU, works on the first day, improves with every edit. Revisit per-user adapters only once we have thousands of users and a measured quality ceiling.

A6–A7

Fixed tab scheme, or negotiated with the user?

Judgment call

Asking a new user "what folders do you want?" is a bad first interaction — they don't know yet, and it front-loads work before any value has been delivered. But a fixed scheme fits nobody exactly.

Google's Priority Inbox paper makes the underlying point well: importance is highly personal, so they learn a per-user model rather than shipping one global definition. The same applies to categories.

Recommendation — observe, then propose

On connection, run the dry-run scan and then propose a scheme derived from what's actually in their mailbox: "You have a lot of mail from your university, from three banks, and from about 40 newsletters. I suggest: University · Money · Newsletters · Everything else. Rename or merge any of these." One message, concrete, derived from their real data, and they only have to react rather than invent. Free-text renaming, no forms.

A8

What does "sort 1,900 emails" look like as a flow the user trusts?

Judgment call

The risk is obvious: a user who lets an agent touch 1,900 messages and gets one wrong outcome will never trust it again. The design has to make the first run feel reversible before it feels impressive.

Recommendation — four properties
  • Dry run first. Nothing moves until the user says go. Show the plan, not the result.
  • Group by sender, never by message. "412 emails from 6 newsletters" is one decision; 412 messages is 412 decisions.
  • Archive to a reviewable label, never delete. 30-day undo, one command to reverse everything.
  • Report in counts, not lists. "1,900 → 240, 41 lists unsubscribed" is the demo. A wall of message subjects is not.
A9

What defensibly makes an email red, amber or green?

Strong evidence

This is the item where published research helps most. Google's The Learning Behind Gmail Priority Inbox (Aberdeen & Pacovsky) describes their production system, and the design is instructive:

  • They rank by the probability the user will act on the message — not by an abstract notion of importance. That reframing is the whole trick, because "will they act?" is measurable and "is it important?" is not.
  • Three feature families: social (how much of this sender's mail does this user read?), content (headers and terms correlated with acting), and thread (did the user start this thread, have they replied in it?).
  • The model is simple logistic regression — a global model trained across all users plus a per-user additive correction. Not a neural network, not an LLM.

That last point is worth dwelling on. Google, with unlimited resources, ships logistic regression for this. It is cheap, interpretable, and it updates continuously as the user behaves.

Recommendation

Copy the architecture. Severity = P(user acts) from a small logistic model, combined with deadline proximity from A4's object axis. Red = high P(act) or a deadline inside the warning window. Amber = one of the two. Green = neither.

Two things fall out of this for free: it explains itself ("you reply to 80% of mail from this sender"), and it needs no LLM. Add the per-user correction from day one — importance genuinely is personal, and Google's paper is the evidence.

A10

What lead times should reminders default to?

Needs measurement

Reminder research offers one useful principle: the longer the lead time, the less precision matters. A reminder a week before an event can arrive at any convenient hour; a reminder fifteen minutes before must be exact. Beyond that, published "best time to send" data conflicts badly and is mostly drawn from marketing email, which is not our problem.

Recommendation — ship defaults, then learn
Object typeFirst reminderThen
Meeting / call30 min beforeOnce only
Deliverable / document due1 day beforeMorning of
Bill / payment2 days before1 day, then morning of
Tax / legal filing7 days before3 days, 1 day, morning of
Renewal / subscription3 days beforeOnce only

Then learn from dismissals: if a user consistently dismisses the 7-day tax reminder but acts on the 1-day one, shorten their lead time automatically and tell them you did. This is far better than a settings screen, and it's the same personalization principle as A9.

A11–A12

How should reminders escalate, and how should they be dismissed?

Judgment call

The evidence on interruption is that users respond in a burst in the ten minutes after a notification, and that whether they respond at all depends mostly on whether the moment was convenient. That argues for fewer, better-timed notifications rather than more insistent ones.

Recommendation

Escalation: halve the interval each time, cap at three notifications for any single item, and never send between 10pm and 7am unless the deadline is inside that window. An item that has been notified three times and still not acted on moves into the daily brief instead of firing again — persistent nagging trains people to ignore you.

Dismissal has three levels, and the user should be offered all three inline: this one · this type · this sender. "This type" is what feeds the rule generalization in A14.

A13

How do we configure without a settings screen?

Judgment call

Sir named Gmail's settings as the anti-pattern: comprehensive, complex, and consequently unused. The insight worth extracting is that a settings screen is a failure to infer. Every option on it is a question the product could not answer on its own.

Recommendation — three sources of configuration, none of them a form
  • Defaults that are right for most people (A10's table).
  • Corrections — the user dismisses, edits, or reverses something, and we adjust. This is the main channel and it requires no interface at all.
  • Conversation — the user says what they want in a sentence, and we translate it to a rule diff (A14).

The web dashboard shows the resulting rules as readable sentences with a delete button. It's a viewer, not an editor. Nobody has to visit it.

A14

When the user changes one rule, how do we find the others of the same type?

Judgment call

Sir raised this specifically and it is the hardest product problem in the brief. "Change these types of notifications" requires knowing what "type" means to this user — and getting it wrong in either direction is bad. Too narrow and nothing improves; too broad and you silently break reminders they wanted.

Options considered
ApproachProblem
Ask the model to rewrite all rulesUnbounded blast radius, unauditable, and expensive.
Change only the exact rule namedFails the request — the user said "these types", plural.
Cluster by embedding similarityOpaque. You cannot explain to a user why dentist grouped with doctor but not with lab test.
Cluster on the A4 object axis, then confirmExplainable, bounded, reversible.
Recommendation

Because A4 already tags every rule with an object type, "the same type" has a concrete definition: same object type, same speech act. The model's only job is to identify which cell of the grid the user meant. Then show the cluster and ask:

Found 4 rules of type appointment → changing 48h to 2h.
Affects: doctor, dentist, physiotherapy, lab tests.
[apply all] [only doctor] [cancel]

Never generalize silently. The confirmation step is what makes an unreliable inference safe, and it costs the user one tap.

A15–A16

Calendar handling, and text input versus buttons

Settled

Calendar is fully supported on both platforms (see B). For input: this is not an either/or. Always accept free text; always offer buttons for the two or three most likely actions. Buttons make the common case one tap; free text makes the uncommon case possible. Telegram and WhatsApp both support inline buttons, so this costs nothing.

B13–B14

Can we process a 20,000-message backlog without hitting rate limits?

Settled

Yes, comfortably. Gmail's published quota is 250 units per user per second, 15,000 units per user per minute, against a project ceiling of 1,200,000 units per minute and 1 billion units per day. Different calls cost different amounts — a label listing is 1 unit, sending a draft is 100.

OperationRough cost20,000 messages
List message IDs (batched)~5 units/pagenegligible
Fetch metadata per message~5 units~100,000 units
Apply label / archive~5 units~100,000 units
Finding

Around 200,000 units for a full backfill and cleanup, against a 15,000-unit-per-minute per-user ceiling — roughly 13 minutes, and less if we fetch metadata only rather than full bodies (which we should, since S0 only needs the envelope). The headline demo is quota-safe. The project ceiling is the one to watch as user count grows: 1.2M units/minute means roughly 80 concurrent backfills, so onboarding must be queued rather than run on demand.

B15

Gmail's own filters, or our own rules, for scope control?

Judgment call

Gmail can create native server-side filters via settings.basic. Tempting — they run without us. But they're invisible to us once created, they can't express "only run the agent on this sender", and they'd leave residue in the user's account if they ever leave.

Recommendation

Keep scope rules in our own rule store. They're ours to reason about, they work identically across all four provider tiers, and uninstalling us leaves no trace in the user's mailbox — which is itself a trust feature worth advertising.

B16–B17

Outlook personal versus work accounts; and the capability matrix

Strong evidence

Microsoft Graph serves both Outlook.com personal accounts and Microsoft 365 work accounts through the same API surface, but tenant administrators of work accounts can restrict third-party app consent — so a work account may simply refuse, through no fault of ours. Handle that as a first-class error state with a clear message, not a crash.

Action

Write the capability matrix as a real file in the repo before the second connector is built. Columns: push, labels, categories, threads, server_search, calendar, unsubscribe. Every connector implements a capabilities() method returning it, and the product asks that method rather than checking which provider it's talking to. This is a small discipline that prevents a large rewrite.

CNotification channel7 items

The channel question is settled by a legal fact rather than a technical one.

C1–C2

What does WhatsApp Business API actually require in Pakistan?

Settled

SECP or firm registration, an FBR NTN, and a live website with a privacy policy and contact page, all matching Meta Business Manager records exactly. Self-filled forms and invoices are rejected. Review takes anywhere from 24 hours to 15 working days; most clear within about two weeks when documents match exactly.

The blocker

This is a company-formation requirement, not an engineering one. A student project without a registered legal entity cannot obtain WhatsApp Business API. No amount of engineering effort changes this.

Separately, messages we initiate must be pre-approved templates billed per message; but once the user replies, a 24-hour service window opens in which free-form messages are free. So the eventual cost design is one template per user per day to open the window, with everything else riding inside it.

C3–C4

Does Telegram hold up as the primary channel, and what does it cost?

Settled

Comfortably, and nothing. Telegram's limits: about 1 message per second to any single user, about 20 per minute to a group, and a global ceiling of 30 messages per second across all chats — raisable to 1,000/sec via paid broadcasts, which requires a large Stars balance and which we will not need.

What this means at our scale

At 10,000 users receiving three messages a day, that's 30,000 messages daily — an average of 0.35 per second against a 30/sec ceiling. Ample.

The only real constraint is bursts: if every user's morning brief fires at 08:00, 10,000 messages at 30/sec takes about six minutes. The fix is trivial and should be built in from V0 — jitter the send window across ten minutes and respect each user's own timezone, which is better product behaviour anyway.

Cost comparison at 10,000 users: Telegram $0. WhatsApp at one utility template per user per day, at South Asian rates, roughly $8–20 per day — real but manageable, and only once there's revenue.

C5–C7

Other channels, rich interaction, and unofficial WhatsApp routes

Settled
  • Rich interaction is available on both: Telegram inline keyboards and WhatsApp interactive messages both give us send / edit / ignore as buttons. Build the approval UI against a small abstraction of "message plus up to three actions" and both render it.
  • Native push arrives with the mobile app in V3 — the only channel that works when the user has no chat app open.
  • Email-to-self is a genuinely useful zero-cost fallback for users on no chat platform, and it costs us almost nothing since we already have send permission.
  • Slack and Discord are cheap adapters worth adding if a pilot cohort asks; not before.
  • Unofficial WhatsApp libraries (web automation, reverse-engineered clients) violate Meta's terms and risk banning the user's number. Ruled out. Not worth discussing further.
DClient surfaces7 items
D1

One backend with thin clients, or several agents?

Settled

One backend. Mail arrives while the phone is asleep, so the agent is server-side regardless; once that's true, the chat channel is a free client covering every platform. Field Manual §09.

D2

React Native or Flutter for the V3 mobile app?

Strong evidence

Both are production-grade in 2026 and both handle push notifications through Firebase Cloud Messaging and APNs with equivalent reliability. On pure technical merit there is no decisive winner, which means the decision should be made on something else.

The something else is hiring. The JavaScript talent pool is roughly 10–15× larger than the Dart pool, and the consistent advice is that a team which cannot reliably hire Dart developers in its geography should not choose Flutter regardless of technical fit.

Recommendation

React Native. Not because it's technically better, but because it shares a language with the backend (see G7), shares it with the reference implementation we're learning from (Inbox Zero is TypeScript), and is hireable in Pakistan. One language across the whole stack is worth more than a marginal rendering advantage.

D3

Could a Chrome extension do the work instead of a server?

Settled — no

Worth checking properly, because "just build an extension" is a tempting way to avoid server costs and OAuth verification. Manifest V3 rules it out:

  • No persistent background page. The background is a service worker that Chrome terminates whenever it's idle. An agent that must react to mail arriving cannot live there.
  • Content scripts can't reach privileged APIs directly and must message the service worker — which may not be running.
  • No window, no document, no localStorage in the worker; eval is banned outright.
Recommendation

An extension can only ever be a view onto a server-side agent, never the agent itself. That makes it low-value once the chat channel exists. Deferred past V3, and now we can say exactly why rather than as a matter of taste.

D4–D7

Desktop apps, push infrastructure, an SDK, and reading mail on mobile

Judgment call
  • Desktop: skip entirely. Telegram and WhatsApp both have excellent desktop clients that we get for free. Building an Electron or Tauri app to deliver notifications that Telegram Desktop already delivers is pure cost.
  • Push: FCM plus APNs, equivalent on either framework, arriving with the V3 app.
  • SDK: not now. It freezes internal interfaces we still need to change weekly.
  • Reading mail on mobile (sir asked for this): the honest answer is that we shouldn't build a mail reader — that's an email client, which is explicitly out of scope. What we do instead is make reading unnecessary: the brief carries the summary, and any message we mention carries a deep link that opens it in the user's own Gmail or Outlook app. We borrow their reader rather than building one.
ECost & optimization10 items

Two findings here are worth real money: measured prompt compression, and the fact that model self-confidence is not a usable routing signal.

E1–E3

The cascade cost model, current pricing, caching and batching

Settled

Field Manual §05. Naive ≈ $54/user/month; cascade ≈ $0.91–2.50. Caching bills repeated prefixes at roughly a tenth; batch processing is half price for non-interactive work.

E4

Should the cascade be provider-agnostic?

Strong evidence

Frontier APIs currently sit around $2–15 per million tokens in/out; open-weight hosted APIs (Llama, DeepSeek and similar) run roughly $0.07–0.90. That's a 10–30× spread, and it matters more than it looks: our S2 tier is small enough that even the expensive end is affordable, but a cheaper tier could absorb the ambiguous-triage escalations that don't need top-tier writing quality.

Recommendation

Put S2 behind a provider interface from the first commit, exactly as we do for S1. Not to switch providers immediately — quality for drafting is worth paying for — but so that routing within S2 becomes possible: a cheap open-weight model for escalated triage, a frontier model for drafting. That's a second cost lever we get for the price of one interface.

E5

How much can we compress a prompt before quality suffers?

Strong evidence

This is better researched than expected. Microsoft's LLMLingua family is the state of the art, and it works by using a small language model to score which tokens carry information and dropping the rest — which fits our architecture exactly, because we already have a small model in S1.

CompressionCost reductionAccuracy impact
2–3×~80%Under 5%
5–7×85–90%5–15%
Above 8×>90%Measurable degradation

Their budget controller compresses different parts of a prompt differently — instructions lightly (10–20%), examples heavily (60–80%), the actual question barely at all (0–10%). That maps neatly onto our prompt structure: persona block, style exemplars, and the email itself.

Recommendation

Target 2–3× compression and no more. That's the point where the curve is still nearly free. Combined with quote-stripping (which is lossless — quoted history is genuinely redundant) this should take a 3,000-token thread to under 1,000. Compress exemplars hard, the incoming message barely at all.

E6

What confidence signal should trigger escalation from S1 to S2?

Strong evidence

This is more subtle than it appears, and getting it wrong is expensive in both directions. The 2026 literature on cascade routing is unusually clear on two points:

  • A model's self-reported confidence is poorly calibrated. Language models produce fluent, authoritative, high-probability text while being wrong. Asking a model "how sure are you?" is close to useless as a routing signal.
  • Calibration matters more than router sophistication. Theoretical work finds that excess cost is controlled by how well-calibrated the confidence estimate is — and that "confidently wrong" is the binding failure mode, not "uncertain".
Recommendation — and it settles an earlier open question

Use the calibrated probability from a classification head, not a generative model's self-assessment. A classifier trained with a proper scoring rule gives a genuinely calibrated number; a language model asked to rate itself does not.

This is a concrete argument for the encoder-plus-head design over a small generative model at S1 (see H6): the encoder gives us a usable routing signal for free, and the generative model does not. Calibrate on a held-out set, then set the threshold to hit the S2 volume the budget allows — and re-calibrate as S1 improves, so escalation volume falls automatically.

E7

What fraction of a real inbox is decidable from the envelope alone?

Needs measurement — highest priority

The entire cost architecture assumes about 70%. This number cannot be looked up; it has to be measured, and it's the single most valuable open item in the project. Here is the method, so it can be done in an afternoon.

Method
  1. Pull 1,000 recent messages via the Gmail API using format=metadata — headers only, no bodies. Costs about 5,000 quota units.
  2. For each, record: sender domain, presence of List-Unsubscribe, Precedence, authentication results, whether it's in a thread, and whether you have ever replied to that sender.
  3. Hand-label each as bulk / notification / needs-a-human. That's the tedious part — about two hours.
  4. Write the simplest possible rule set over the recorded signals and measure: what percentage is classified correctly with no body read, and — much more importantly — what is the false-positive rate on "needs a human"?

The headline number is nice. The false-positive rate is the one that matters, because a message wrongly filed as bulk is a message the user never sees. Target under 1%, and where the rules are unsure, pass to S1 rather than guessing.

E8–E9

Self-hosting, and what we can charge

Settled

Break-even for self-hosted inference sits around 100M tokens/day once operations are counted at the usual 3–5× multiple on raw compute; we'd need roughly 5,000 active users to reach it. Use the API for S2. This does not apply to S1, where a 66M-parameter classifier on CPU has no meaningful operational burden.

Market pricing runs $14–30/month (Field Manual §08) against a $1–3 cost to serve, which supports a two-price strategy: near $10 in Western markets, near $3–5 locally.

E10

What transfers from the LiteRT work?

Judgment call

Sir pointed at your own experience here, so it's worth being specific about what carries over rather than gesturing at it. Three things transfer directly:

  • Quantization intuition. Knowing what int8 and 4-bit actually cost in accuracy is exactly the judgment S1 needs (see H5).
  • Operator-level thinking about latency. On-device work teaches you to count what actually runs, which is the right instinct for a per-message hot path.
  • The habit of measuring instead of assuming. The single most valuable transfer, and the reason E7 and F3 are framed as measurements rather than claims.

What does not transfer is the deployment target: LiteRT is about squeezing a model onto a handset, and our S1 runs on a server. The techniques are similar; the constraint is not.

FPrivacy & security10 items

One uncomfortable finding here that needs saying: the zero-retention commitment we'd like to make is not straightforwardly available to a project our size.

F1–F2

Staged minimization, and detecting sensitive mail from the envelope

Settled

Field Manual §06. S0 sees envelopes, S1 runs on our hardware, S2 sees only redacted content. OTP and banking mail is identified from sender, subject shape and body length without the body being read.

F3

Which redaction tool, and how good is it really?

Recommendation — layered, with a safety valve

Presidio as the base (regex layer plus NER layer), GLiNER added as a recognizer for the entity types Presidio covers poorly, and a confidence floor: if the redactor's confidence on any span is low, the message does not go to S2 at all — it's handled by S1 and the user is told the draft is lower quality because the content was sensitive. Degrading quality is an acceptable failure; leaking an identity is not.

Before claiming this works

Build a labelled evaluation set of 200–300 real emails with every name, number and identifier marked by hand, and measure the false-negative rate — how often a real identity survives redaction. A redactor that catches 95% is not 95% good; it means one email in twenty leaks. This measurement gates the privacy claim, and the claim must not be made publicly until it exists.

F4

Can we actually get a zero-retention commitment?

Problem — needs a decision

This one deserves honesty because it complicates the pitch. Standard API terms across the major providers retain inputs and outputs for roughly 7 to 30 days for abuse monitoring. True zero-retention is generally available only through an enterprise agreement — a contract addendum requiring approval, and some model families are excluded from it even then.

What this means for us

A student project or an early-stage company cannot simply switch on zero retention. So "no data is retained by our AI provider" is a claim we currently cannot make, and making it anyway would be the kind of thing that destroys trust permanently when discovered.

Recommendation

Say what is true instead, and note that our architecture makes the true statement unusually strong: "Content sent to our AI provider is redacted of names and identifiers before it leaves, is retained by them for up to 30 days for abuse monitoring under their standard terms, and is contractually never used for training. We are working toward a zero-retention agreement."

Because of tokenized egress (§06), what sits in that 30-day window is a de-identified skeleton rather than someone's mail — which is a better position than most competitors are actually in, whatever their marketing says. Pursue the ZDR addendum once there's a company and revenue.

F5

Encryption, key management, tenant isolation

Judgment call

Standard practice, applied properly. Nothing exotic is needed, and inventing something exotic would be a mistake.

  • OAuth refresh tokens are the crown jewels — they grant standing mailbox access. Encrypt them at the application layer with a key from a managed key service, not just at rest in the database. A database dump alone must not yield usable tokens.
  • TLS everywhere, full-disk encryption, and per-user row-level isolation with the user ID on every query path.
  • Token revocation on disconnect must be immediate and verified — call the provider's revoke endpoint, don't just delete our copy.
  • Secrets in a manager, never in environment files committed anywhere.
F6–F7

Proving non-access, and what actually earns trust

Strong evidence

Four levels of proof, in increasing strength: policy (weak, everyone has one), transparency via the Data Passport (checkable), open-sourcing the privacy gateway (verifiable), and hardware attestation inside a trusted execution environment (cryptographic). The last is now practical — confidential-computing GPUs run at 95–99% of native speed with a 2–5% throughput overhead and a one-to-three-second attestation cost at startup, available on major clouds today.

Recommendation

Ship levels 2 and 3 in V2; name level 4 as the roadmap. On earning trust in practice, three things do most of the work and all are cheap: dry-run the first pass so the user sees the plan before anything moves; never delete, so every mistake is recoverable; and start scoped, so a cautious user can point it at one sender and watch it behave before widening.

F8

What data protection law actually applies to us?

Strong evidence

A genuinely surprising finding: Pakistan has no comprehensive data protection law in force. The Personal Data Protection Bill has been drafted and redrafted since 2023 but has not been enacted. What exists today is PECA, which is a cybercrime statute rather than a privacy framework.

The draft bill matters anyway, because it signals where things are going, and its direction is restrictive: three tiers of data, with "critical personal data" required to stay on servers inside Pakistan, other personal data transferable abroad only with explicit consent, and a National Commission to enforce it.

Recommendation

Two consequences worth planning for now, neither expensive:

  • Design for data residency — keep the storage layer region-configurable from the start. If the bill passes with localization intact, a product that cannot run its database in Pakistan becomes illegal to sell there overnight.
  • Build to GDPR standards regardless. It is the strictest regime we might face, it's what any Western customer or partner will ask about, and our "store structure, not content" rule already satisfies most of it — deletion on request is nearly trivial when you never stored the content.
F9–F10

Threat model, and how much to say about an OTP

Judgment call

Threat model, written out: if the database leaks, the attacker gets vectors, labels and metadata — not mail. If OAuth tokens leak, the attacker gets mailbox access, which is why F5 treats them separately and more carefully. If provider logs leak, they contain redacted skeletons. The worst case is a compromise of the privacy gateway itself, which is why it should be small, isolated, and eventually published.

On OTPs: even saying "you have a code from HBL" is a signal — it tells anyone who sees the notification that you bank there. Default to naming the sender but never the code, allow the user to turn even that off, and never include OTP content in a digest that might be read over someone's shoulder.

G5

An agent framework, or plain code?

Strong evidence

Worth understanding the distinction properly, because the two things that sound similar do different jobs. An agent framework defines what the agent does. A durable execution engine ensures the work survives a crash. Popular agent frameworks checkpoint state, but a checkpoint is not durable execution — a run lives in one process, and if the process dies the run dies with it. The checkpoint preserves data, not execution.

Why this matters specifically to us

Our approval loop waits for a human — possibly overnight. An approval lost because a server restarted is a visible product failure, and one the user will never forgive because they'll assume the reply was sent. Whatever we build, the pending-approval state must be durable in a database, not in process memory.

Recommendation

Plain code plus a durable queue. Agent frameworks exist to manage the unpredictability of free-roaming agents — unpredictability we deliberately removed in §04. Adopting one adds a dependency to solve a problem we designed away. Plain code is simpler to debug, cheaper to audit, and auditability is part of what we sell.

G6

Strong evidence

We need three things: background jobs (ingestion, backfill), delayed jobs (every reminder is a job scheduled for a future time), and durability (G5).

G7

Which language and runtime?

Judgment call

The one genuine argument for Python is the machine-learning ecosystem — S1's training and evaluation live there naturally.

G8

Idempotency and undo

Judgment call

Non-negotiable in a system that mutates someone's mailbox, and cheap if designed in from the start.

  • Every action gets a deterministic key: (user, message_id, action_type). Replaying the same event does nothing the second time.
  • Every action writes an audit row before executing, with the prior state recorded — that row is what makes undo possible.
  • Undo is a first-class operation, not a feature: "undo the last hour" and "undo everything from the cleanup" must both work.
  • Gmail's history API can deliver notifications out of order or repeat them; the queue must be ordered per user and tolerant of duplicates.

Recommendation — read it in week one

G10–G12

Model abstraction, the three-tier data flow, and "lightweight but scalable"

Settled

Abstraction: S1 and S2 both sit behind interfaces; the router is the only component that knows both exist. Swapping either touches nothing else.

Data flow (sir asked for this explicitly): the three tiers never talk to each other — they all talk to the router. message → S0 → router → S1 (returns answer + calibrated confidence) → router → privacy gateway → S2 → back. Every hop writes to the passport log and the distillation log. Crucially the router escalates on confidence, not category, so S2 volume falls automatically as S1 improves.

Lightweight versus scalable — these pull in opposite directions, so name the resolution: the core is lightweight (a queue, a rules engine, a router, a small model — all of which run on one modest machine), and scale comes from running more copies of stateless workers against a shared PostgreSQL. No distributed system, no microservices, no Kubernetes until there's a measured reason. The architecture scales by addition rather than by rearchitecting.

HThe small-model track11 items

The best finding in this track: there is a large public email corpus we can train and evaluate on before we have a single user.

H1–H2

Is 10–100M parameters realistic, and what's out there?

Settled

Yes for classification, no for generation. DistilBERT is ~66M parameters with F1 between 0.93 and 0.95 across classification benchmarks and roughly 60% faster than full BERT on CPU; MiniLM is another 1.5–2× faster again, under 5ms per sample. Anything that must write needs 0.5–1.7B — Qwen3-0.6B, Gemma 3, Llama 3.2 1B, SmolLM2. ModernBERT is the current option for longer documents.

H3

How do we benchmark candidates before we have users?

Strong evidence

This looked like a chicken-and-egg problem and isn't. The Enron corpus — roughly 500,000 real messages from about 150 users, released during a legal investigation — is the standard public dataset for email research, and it's what the speech-act taxonomy work in A4 was built on.

Recommendation — this unblocks the whole track

Build the S1 evaluation harness on Enron in week one, before any user exists:

  1. Label a few thousand Enron messages against the A4 two-axis taxonomy — the existing intent-detection work gives a starting point.
  2. Train and compare MiniLM, DistilBERT and a small generative model on the same split.
  3. Measure accuracy, calibration (E6), and CPU latency per message.

Caveat worth stating: Enron is corporate American email from the early 2000s. It will not represent a Pakistani university inbox, and the model will need re-tuning on real data. But it is enough to choose an architecture and build the harness, which is what blocks everything else. Getting the harness right is worth more than getting the first model right.

H4–H5

Which runtime, and how much quantization?

Strong evidence

Runtime. ONNX Runtime measurably outperforms llama.cpp on CPU — reported at over 4.75× on prompt throughput and around 1.5× on token generation for quantized models. And for encoder models like MiniLM and DistilBERT, ONNX is the natural home anyway; llama.cpp is built for generative transformers.

Quantization. Q4_K_M is the accepted default for generative models — roughly halving the memory footprint versus 8-bit, and because decoding is memory-bandwidth-bound, the smaller footprint usually increases throughput. For the encoder classifiers, int8 is standard and the accuracy cost is negligible.

Recommendation

ONNX Runtime with int8 for the S1 classifiers. If a generative small model is later needed for summaries, run it as a separate service under llama.cpp with Q4_K_M, or ONNX if the model converts cleanly. Benchmark on the actual target machine — CPU inference varies enormously with instruction-set support, and published numbers assume modern server cores.

H6

Encoder-plus-head, or a small generative model, for triage?

Strong evidence

E6 effectively settled this. An encoder with a classification head gives a calibrated probability — a number that means something and can be thresholded for routing. A generative model asked to classify gives text, and its self-reported confidence is known to be poorly calibrated.

Recommendation

Encoder plus head for anything that routes. It's smaller, faster, more accurate on fixed label sets, and — decisively — it produces the confidence signal the router needs. Reserve a generative small model for jobs that genuinely require free text: short summaries and rule diffs.

H8

Is it legal to train on data derived from user mailboxes?

Needs a real answer

Google's Workspace policy prohibits using API-derived data to develop or train generalized, non-personalized models. The wording matters: it targets general-purpose models, and appears to leave room for per-user or per-tenant personalization. But "appears to" is not good enough to bet a product on.

  • Frame the output as personalization: per-tenant adapters rather than one global model, which is also better for quality.
  • Seek explicit opt-in consent for training use, separately from the main consent flow.
  • Get a written legal opinion before the first training run on real data. Enron (H3) is unencumbered and covers all development work until then.

I8–I9

What's the state of the art in agentic systems, and what other benefits exist?

Strong evidence

The pattern catalogue for 2026 agentic systems converges on a handful of things — planning, tool use, reflection, multi-agent delegation, and human-in-the-loop as a cross-cutting modifier. The consistent lesson across the production write-ups is that the challenge has shifted from capability to control: the named failure modes are infinite reflection loops, hallucinated tool calls, and runaway token spend, and the patterns exist to prevent them rather than to add power.

Our design already reflects that lesson — a tiny typed tool surface, no destructive operations, deterministic control flow, and mandatory approval on anything outward-facing. Worth saying explicitly in the meeting, because it shows the architecture came from the literature rather than from caution.

Additional benefits worth exploring, beyond sorting and automation:

  • Commitment tracking — "you promised three people you'd send something this week." The commit speech act in A4 gives us this almost free, and it's the feature people describe wanting when asked.
  • Response-time intelligence — "you usually reply to your supervisor within two hours; this one has been sitting for two days."
  • Relationship memory — surfacing the last thread with someone before you reply.
  • Leaving cleanly — an export of everything we know about you, and an uninstall that leaves no residue in the mailbox (see B15). A trust feature that doubles as a differentiator.

J4–J6

Watch renewal, budgets, and rate-limit strategy

Settled
  • Watch renewal is the highest-risk operational task in the system, because its failure is silent on both ends — mail arrives, we hear nothing, and nothing raises an error anywhere. Renew daily rather than weekly, and run a heartbeat that alarms if any user produces no notifications for an unusually long period given their history.
  • Per-user monthly token budget enforced in the router; on exhaustion, degrade to S1 quality rather than fail or overspend silently.
  • Rate limits: exponential backoff honouring Retry-After, a per-user token bucket sized below Gmail's published ceiling, and a project-level bucket below the per-project one. Backfills run in a separate low-priority lane so one user's 50,000-message import cannot starve everyone else's live mail.

K3

What must not be hard-coded to email — today?

Strong evidence

This is the item with consequences for V0, which is why it belongs in this document rather than a future one. Five naming and modelling decisions, none of which cost anything now and all of which are expensive to change later:

  • The core object is an Item, not an Email. An email is one kind of item; so is a calendar invite, so is a message.
  • The taxonomy (A4) is speech act plus object — deliberately not email-specific. "Someone is requesting something about a payment" is true of a WhatsApp message too.
  • The rule store keys on item properties, not on Gmail concepts. A rule that mentions "label" is an email rule; a rule that mentions "category" generalizes.
  • The approval loop takes an Action, not a Draft. Sending a reply is one action; declining a meeting is another.
  • The connector interface is a source of items with a capability set (B17), which is exactly the shape a future WhatsApp or document connector needs.
Recommendation

Adopt this naming from the first commit. It costs nothing today — the words are just different — and it is the difference between expanding later and rewriting later. It's also a good thing to be able to say in the meeting: it shows you heard the line about "moving on" and designed for it rather than nodding at it.
