Chapter 01

The idea, made concrete

The brief was an abstract: an agent that reads your mail, learns your tone, drafts replies, asks you on WhatsApp, cleans your junk, and warns you about deadlines. Those are features. Here is the product they add up to, and the single decision that makes it a product rather than a pile of features.

The problem, stated properly

Email is not broken because it's hard to read. It's broken because of the volume of decisions it demands. A typical professional inbox takes in over a hundred messages a day. Perhaps eight of them need a real reply. Perhaps three carry a deadline. The rest is noise — but you can only know which is which by looking at all of it. That is the actual cost of email: not the writing, the sorting.

Every existing tool attacks the writing. Gmail suggests replies. Superhuman makes you faster at processing. Shortwave adds better search. All of them assume you are sitting in your inbox, and all of them make you slightly better at being there.

The core decision

We are not making the inbox better. We are making it unnecessary.

The agent works the mailbox on the server. When something genuinely needs you, it comes and finds you on the messaging app you already check forty times a day, tells you what happened, shows you what it plans to do, and asks one question. You answer in one word. You do not open Gmail.

That inversion — the agent comes to you, you don't go to it — is the whole product. It is what makes the WhatsApp piece central rather than a nice extra, and it's what makes "no app to install" true in the first version. Everything else in this document serves it.

The three things that make it defensible

  1. Approval in chat, not in an inbox. Nothing to install, works on every phone and laptop on day one, and the interaction is one message and one word back.
  2. It writes in your words. Not "professional tone" from a dropdown — your actual phrasing, learned from the replies you have already written. §04 explains exactly how, and it turns out to be cheaper than the alternatives, not more expensive.
  3. It can prove what it did and didn't read. Trust is the hardest thing to sell in this category. A competitor can copy a feature in a week; they cannot copy an architecture that structurally cannot see your data.

The pitch, in one sentence

"It cleans out your inbox, tells you what actually matters, drafts the replies in your own words, and messages you to approve them — so you never have to open your email."

If sir asks — "isn't this just another email AI?"

No, and the difference is structural rather than cosmetic. Every competitor is a better inbox. We are an agent that removes the need for one. Practically: they ship a mail client or a browser extension and you work inside it. We ship no client at all — the interface is a chat thread. That changes what we build, what it costs, and who can use it. A user on a low-end Android with 2GB of RAM can use our product; they cannot meaningfully use Superhuman.

The finding that decides the build order

The three highest-value jobs — the vault, the cleanup, and scope control — need no language model at all. They run on the information in an email's envelope: who sent it, whether it carries an unsubscribe header, whether you have ever replied to that sender. So the first version ships in two weeks, demos convincingly, costs nothing per user to run, and sends zero data to any AI provider. That is the opposite of an ambitious project that never finishes.

The one design trap to avoid

Research on human-in-the-loop systems is blunt about the failure mode: the most common mistake is turning the human into a quality-assurance checker, which collapses as soon as volume is real. If the agent asks you to approve forty things a day, you stop reading them and start tapping "send" — which is worse than no agent at all, because now you have automation with the illusion of oversight.

So approval has to be graduated, and this is a product requirement, not a refinement:

  • Silent — filing bulk mail, applying labels. Reported in the morning digest, never asked about.
  • Batched — "3 things need you today", delivered once, not as three interruptions.
  • Individual — anything that sends a message as the user. Always, in every version covered here.
  • Earned autonomy — after the user has approved the same class of reply unchanged twenty times, offer to let that class go automatically. Offer it; never assume it.

What we deliberately don't build

Naming these protects the timeline, and each was a live temptation in the brief:

  • No mail server and no email client. We attach to Gmail and Outlook and stay inside their boundaries.
  • No auto-send without approval, at any confidence level, in any release covered here.
  • No attachment or document processing in V1 — a large separate problem with its own privacy surface.
  • No multi-account, no shared mailboxes, no CRM features, no Chrome extension before V3.
  • No mailbox-wide vector database (§04 explains why this is a considered decision, not an omission).
  • No Proton or iCloud support, ever — §03 explains why that's structural.
Chapter 03

What the platforms allow

Before designing anything, the hard question: what will Google, Microsoft and Meta actually permit a third party to do on a user's behalf? This chapter is the one that changed the plan the most, and it contains the single biggest obstacle in the project.

In plain words — what a "scope" is

When you click "Sign in with Google", you're granting an app permission to do specific things. Each permission is called a scope. gmail.readonly lets an app read your mail; gmail.modify lets it read, label and archive. Google classifies the sensitive ones as restricted scopes, and apps that use them face far heavier requirements — which is where our biggest obstacle comes from.

The provider landscape — four tiers

"Attach to the user's mailbox" can't mean "attach to two vendors." But providers differ so much in what they permit that feature parity across them is impossible. Every mailbox falls into one of four tiers:

Architectural consequence — decide this on day one

Because the tiers differ this much, a connector cannot just be "a mailbox." Every connector declares a capability set — push, labels, categories, threads, server_search, calendar — and the product degrades gracefully against it. A Yahoo user gets folders instead of tabs and a two-minute delay instead of instant, and the interface says so rather than silently doing less. Retrofitting this after building Gmail-first is a rewrite, not a refactor.

The biggest obstacle: Google's CASA requirement

Reading someone's Gmail requires a restricted scope. To launch publicly on one, Google requires verification plus an annual third-party security assessment called CASA — publicly quoted from roughly $500 to $4,500 a year, higher at the top tier, and it must be repeated every twelve months for as long as the app exists. For a student project, that is a wall.

Three ways through it, in order of usefulness:

  1. Testing mode. Google's OAuth "Testing" mode allows up to 100 users on restricted scopes with no verification at all. That covers the entire MVP, the demo, and a real pilot cohort. We need CASA only when we go public.
  2. Internal Workspace install. A Workspace administrator can install an app for their own domain without any public verification. This means the university's @uet.edu.pk domain is the ideal first deployment — and sir's own example of wanting it on just the ORIC mailbox happens to be exactly the deployment that dodges the biggest obstacle in the project.
  3. Scope discipline. The heaviest assessment tier is triggered by full-mailbox access, which includes permanent delete. If our agent can only archive and trash — never permanently delete — then gmail.modify suffices and we stay a tier down. So delete is not in the agent's tool surface. That's a compliance decision as much as a safety one, and it's a good example of the two constraints pointing the same way.

A useful asymmetry: Outlook is easier than Gmail

Microsoft has nothing equivalent to Google's restricted-scope regime. Mail.ReadWrite is a normal user-consentable permission — no admin approval, no annual assessment, no fee. Gmail has the bigger user base; Outlook has the shorter path to shipping. Two operational details: access tokens expire in 60–90 minutes so the offline_access scope is mandatory to get a refresh token, and from 31 December 2026 modifying sensitive properties on non-draft messages moves to elevated, admin-consented permissions — a dated change to design around now rather than discover later.

The notification channel

Sir was right to suspect WhatsApp would be difficult. It is — and the specifics matter.

WhatsApp Business API in Pakistan — the blocker nobody mentions

Getting access requires SECP or firm registration, an FBR NTN, and a live website with a privacy policy, all matching your Meta Business Manager records exactly. Review takes anywhere from 24 hours to 15 working days. A student project without a registered company cannot get WhatsApp Business API. This is not a technical difficulty we can engineer around; it's a legal-entity requirement.

On top of that, messages we initiate must be pre-approved templates billed per message. The redeeming detail: once the user replies, a 24-hour service window opens in which free-form messages are free. So when we do get WhatsApp, the cost design is one template a day to open the window, with the whole day's approvals riding free inside it.

Decision

Build the notification layer channel-agnostic, and ship Telegram first. A Telegram bot needs no business verification, costs nothing, and is about a day's work. Behind one NotificationChannel interface, WhatsApp later becomes a swap rather than a rewrite. This removes a legal dependency from the critical path — and it means the demo can never be blocked by Meta's review queue.

Chapter 04

How the system works

The architecture, explained from first principles. The central choice here is deliberately unfashionable, and it's the choice that makes the cost model and the privacy model possible at all.

In plain words — what "agentic" actually means

A normal program follows steps you wrote. An agent is given a goal and a set of tools, and the model decides which tools to use and in what order. That flexibility is powerful — and it's also why agents are unpredictable. An agent loose on someone's mailbox with a delete tool is a liability, not a feature.

The central principle

Design principle

A deterministic pipeline with models only at the leaves — not a free-roaming agent looping over tools on someone's mailbox.

Every message flows through fixed stages we wrote. A model is called at specific points to answer specific questions ("is this urgent?", "draft a reply"), and its answer feeds back into our code. The model never decides what happens next; our code does. The genuinely agentic part is narrow and deliberate: the router that decides how much intelligence a message deserves, and the approval loop that talks to the user.

The agent's tool surface is therefore deliberately tiny and typed: label, archive, unsubscribe, draft_reply, schedule_reminder, ask_user. There is no delete — which, as §03 showed, also keeps us out of Google's heaviest assessment tier. This is the shape that makes the system auditable, cheap, and safe, and it is what we mean by "abstract design" — the pipeline doesn't care which model sits underneath any given stage.

The nine components

How tone actually works — and why it's cheap

Sir asked for the agent to learn the user's word choice from their own replies. There are three ways to do that, and the research points clearly at one of them.

ApproachHow it worksVerdict
Ask the userA dropdown: formal, friendly, concise.Weak Produces generic output. People are bad at describing their own writing.
Fine-tune per userTrain a small adapter on each person's sent mail.Not first The literature is clear that supervised fine-tuning does not beat in-context examples when style data is scarce — which it always is for one person. Also needs GPUs, per user.
Retrieve examplesFind the user's own past replies most similar to the incoming email, and show them to the model as examples to imitate.This one Works from day one, needs no training, no GPU, and improves every time the user edits a draft.
A number that shapes the design

Style-transfer research finds that 2–5 examples are enough, and imitation plateaus after 4–5 — more examples add cost without adding quality. So we retrieve exactly four or five. That's not a guess to be tuned later; it's a designed-in constant with evidence behind it, and it keeps the prompt small, which keeps it cheap.

On RAG and vector databases

In plain words — what RAG is

Retrieval-Augmented Generation. You convert documents into lists of numbers (embeddings) that capture meaning, store them in a vector database, and when a question comes in you find the closest matches and paste them into the prompt. It's how you give a model knowledge it wasn't trained on.

A vector index over someone's whole mailbox is the wrong tool here, for three reasons worth being able to state: it is the most expensive option; it requires storing exactly the content we've promised not to store; and it solves a problem email doesn't have, because email tasks are thread-scoped, not corpus-scoped. When you reply to a message, the context you need is that thread and your relationship with that sender — not your last ten thousand emails.

Three narrow retrievals do the job instead:

  • Thread-local context — already in hand, free, covers the large majority of drafting needs.
  • Sender memory — one small structured record per correspondent: relationship, usual formality, recent subjects, typical response time.
  • Style exemplars — a small local vector index over the user's own sent mail only. This is the tone mechanism above. Embeddings computed locally; we store the vectors, not the mail.

Natural-language settings, implemented safely

Sir was firm that we must not rebuild Gmail's settings screen, which nobody uses because it's overwhelming. The design that satisfies this without putting a model in charge of anything important:

Settings live as a structured rule store — each rule is data: trigger, condition, action, timing, scope. The model's only job is to translate a sentence into a proposed change to that data. The user sees the change in plain English and confirms it. At runtime, only the rules execute; no model is involved.

stop reminding me 2 days before doctor appointments, 2 hours is enough
Found 4 rules of type medical appointment. Changing lead time from 48h → 2h.

This also affects: dentist, physiotherapy, lab tests.apply · only doctor · cancel

That second line is the hard part sir specifically flagged — working out which other rules fall in the same category. We solve it by clustering rules by their extracted entity type and showing the user the cluster before acting, rather than guessing silently. The user stays in control and the system stays auditable.

Two stack decisions worth making now

Durability, not just orchestration. Our approval loop waits for a human — possibly for hours, possibly overnight. Popular agent frameworks checkpoint state but do not provide durable execution: if the process dies, the run dies with it, and the checkpoint preserves data rather than the workflow. An approval that gets lost because a server restarted is a product failure. So the approval loop must be built on something durable — a persisted job with an external scheduler, or a workflow engine — and that decision belongs at the start, not after the first outage.

Prefer plain code to an agent framework. Our pipeline is deterministic by design (§04, principle). Most agent frameworks exist to manage the unpredictability of free-roaming agents — unpredictability we have deliberately removed. Adopting one would add a dependency to solve a problem we don't have. Plain code plus a durable queue is simpler, cheaper to debug, and easier to audit — and auditability is part of what we're selling.

Chapter 05

The cost architecture

Sir's central constraint: don't send everything to a frontier model, or the product can't be sold. This chapter puts a number on what that constraint is worth, and shows the arithmetic so it can be checked rather than believed.

In plain words — tokens and why they cost money

Language models charge by the token — roughly ¾ of a word. You pay for what you send (input) and what comes back (output), at different rates. A typical email plus its quoted history might be 3,000 tokens. Multiply by a hundred emails a day and thirty days, and you can see how a naive design gets expensive fast.

The principle

The principle

The biggest cost lever is not which model you pick. It is not calling a model at all. Most of what an inbox needs is decidable from the envelope, and most of the rest is decidable by a small model on our own hardware. The frontier model is reserved for the one job where quality is the product: writing the reply.

The arithmetic

Assumptions, stated so they can be challenged: 120 inbound messages a day; about 8 replies a day the user actually wants drafted; roughly 1,800 input and 400 output tokens per draft after stripping and compression.

# Naive — every email, full body, to a frontier model
120 emails × 3,000 tokens × 30 days = 10,800,000 input tokens/month
× $5.00 per million (Opus-tier) = $54 per user per month — input alone. Unsellable.

# Cascade — S0 and S1 absorb 95%, S2 drafts only
8 drafts × 30 days = 240 calls → 432,000 in + 96,000 out
Haiku-tier ($1 / $5): $0.43 + $0.48 = $0.91 per user per month
Sonnet-tier ($2 / $10): $0.86 + $0.96 = $1.82 per user per month
Tiered, hardest 10% escalated: ~$2.50 per user per month

A 20–50× reduction — and, crucially, it lands the product at a price a Pakistani user can pay, not only an American one. Two further levers sit on top:

  • Prompt caching. The persona block, style exemplars and rule set are identical across calls. Cached portions are billed at roughly a tenth of normal input, with five-minute and one-hour lifetimes available.
  • Batch processing at half price for everything non-interactive — nightly re-sorting, weekly digests, and the initial mailbox cleanup.

Should we self-host the frontier model?

Worth answering directly, because it's the obvious question and the obvious answer is wrong. Self-hosted inference on a rented GPU can reach roughly $0.10–0.20 per million tokens, which looks dramatically cheaper than API pricing — until you count operations. Running production inference reliably consumes a meaningful fraction of a senior engineer's time, and the all-in figure is commonly 3–5× the raw compute. The break-even against a premium API typically lands somewhere around 100 million tokens per day.

We are projecting under 20,000 tokens per user per day. We would need 5,000 active users before self-hosting even becomes an argument. Verdict: use the API for S2, revisit at real scale.

Note carefully that this does not apply to S1. A 66-million-parameter classifier is a completely different animal from serving a 70-billion-parameter chat model — it runs in milliseconds on an ordinary CPU with no GPU, no scaling infrastructure, and no operational burden. Self-hosting S1 is obviously right; self-hosting S2 is obviously wrong. Being able to explain why they differ is the point.

Chapter 06

The privacy architecture

Sir asked for a smart angle — something that makes the system oblivious to user data without crippling it. This is it. The insight is that it's the same design as the cost architecture: every byte we don't send is a byte we don't pay for and don't expose.

The four mechanisms

Mechanism 01
Sensitive Vault
OTPs, banking, health and legal mail identified from the envelope alone at S0 — sender lists, subject patterns, and the short-body-with-a-numeric-code shape. Their bodies never enter any model, local or remote. The user gets "6-digit code from HBL — open in Gmail" and nothing more.
Mechanism 02
Minimization by stage
S0 sees envelopes only. S1 sees bodies but runs on our own hardware. S2 sees only what survives redaction. Each stage gets the least data that can do its job, enforced in code rather than policy.
Mechanism 03
Tokenized egress
Before anything reaches a frontier model, a local pass swaps names, addresses, phone numbers, account numbers and amounts for stable placeholders — <PERSON_1>, <AMOUNT_1>. The provider sees the skeleton of a conversation, never an identity. The draft is restored locally before the user reads it.
Mechanism 04
Data Passport
Every handled message carries a receipt: what was read, what left, in what redacted form, to which model, at what cost, under what retention. A real user-facing feature that doubles as our compliance evidence.
The storage rule — the strongest claim we have

We store derived structure, never content. Message IDs, labels, extracted entities, style vectors, the sender graph — yes. Raw bodies — no, beyond a short processing window. If our database is breached, the attacker gets embeddings and metadata, not anybody's mail. That sentence only stays true if we design for it from the first commit; it cannot be bolted on later.

The honest part: redaction is a research risk

Mechanism 03 is the load-bearing one, and it deserves scepticism rather than confidence. The standard open-source tool, Microsoft Presidio, combines regular expressions for structured items (emails, card numbers, national IDs) with a language model for contextual ones (names, organisations). It covers around eighteen entity types, and independent evaluation finds its recall drops when the range of personal data is broader than that set. Newer approaches such as GLiNER can be plugged in to improve coverage.

What this means for us

We must measure our own false-negative rate before claiming this works. A redaction system that catches 95% of names is not 95% good — it means one in twenty emails leaks a real identity, and the promise is broken. The mitigation is layered rather than singular: regex for structured data (near-perfect on account numbers and phone numbers), a model for names, and a fallback that routes anything the redactor flags as low-confidence back down to S1 instead of out to S2. When in doubt, don't send it. Build the evaluation set before building the feature.

How do you prove you didn't read something?

Sir asked this directly, and it's a sharper question than it first appears. Saying "we don't read your mail" is what every company says. Four answers, in increasing order of strength:

  1. Policy — a privacy policy that commits to it. Weakest; everyone has one.
  2. Transparency — the Data Passport shows, per message, exactly what left. Much stronger, because it's checkable against reality.
  3. Open source — publish the privacy gateway so anyone can read what it sends. Strong, and cheap for us since the gateway is small and isn't where our value lives.
  4. Hardware attestation — run inference inside a trusted execution environment, where the hardware cryptographically signs a statement of exactly what code is running. The user's client verifies that signature. This is now practical: confidential-computing GPUs run at 95–99% of normal speed with a one-to-three-second startup cost for attestation, and are available on major clouds today.
The position to take

Ship levels 2 and 3 in V2 — the passport and an open-sourced gateway. Name level 4 as the roadmap. That combination is genuinely stronger than anything in the category, and it turns "trust us" into "check for yourself", which is the only version of a privacy claim that means anything.

Compliance obligations

  • Google Workspace policy — must not train generalized models on API-derived data, and must say so in our privacy policy. Non-negotiable, and already our intended design.
  • Zero-retention API tiers — we should use, and be able to point to, a provider commitment that prompts aren't retained or trained on.
  • CASA — the annual assessment from §03, once we're public. Every mechanism in this chapter is also evidence for it, so the privacy work pays for itself twice.
  • Data protection law — GDPR if we ever have European users, Pakistan's data protection regime locally. Both require deletion on request, which our "store structure, not content" rule makes almost trivial to honour.
Chapter 07

The small-model track

The second recording asked for a small language model — 10 to 100 million parameters, running on CPU, handling the routine work so the frontier model handles only what it must. Here is what's real about that, what isn't, and the sequencing that makes it achievable.

In plain words — parameters, and the two kinds of small model

A model's parameters are the numbers it learned during training; more parameters usually means more capability and more compute. The critical distinction is between understanding and generating. Sorting a message into a category is understanding — a small model does it well. Writing a fluent reply is generating — that needs far more. These are different jobs with different size requirements, and conflating them is where most people go wrong.

Sir's instinct was right — for the right jobs

At 10–100 million parameters you cannot get a usable generative model. But we don't need one there, and the classification jobs we actually route to S1 fit that range precisely:

JobRight sizeCandidateMeasured performance
Category, urgency, is-this-bulk33–66MMiniLM or DistilBERT with a classification head, fine-tuned on our own labelled trafficDistilBERT: ~66M parameters, F1 between 0.93 and 0.95 across classification benchmarks, ~60% faster than full BERT on CPU. MiniLM is another 1.5–2× faster again, under 5ms per message.
PII detection for redaction~100MspaCy-based (Presidio) or GLiNERNeeds our own evaluation — see §06.
Style-exemplar retrieval~100–300MSentence embedding modelMilliseconds on CPU; runs once per sent message, not per query.
Summaries, rule diffs, extraction0.5–1.7BQwen3-0.6B, Gemma 3, Llama 3.2 1B, SmolLM2 — quantizedAbove sir's range, but this is where generation genuinely starts working.

So the honest answer to "is 10–100M enough?" is: yes for the classification work, which is most of the volume; no for anything that writes, which needs roughly ten times that. That's a good answer to give — it confirms the instinct, adds the precision, and shows you checked rather than agreed.

In plain words — quantization and distillation

Quantization stores a model's numbers at lower precision — 4 bits instead of 16 — making it about four times smaller and faster, with a small accuracy cost. It's what lets a 1B model run on a normal CPU.

Distillation is training a small model to imitate a large one. The large model (the "teacher") produces answers; the small model (the "student") learns to produce the same answers. You end up with something far cheaper that's nearly as good on that specific task.

The sequencing — the part that matters most

Don't train a model first. Collect the data first.

Every single call we make to the frontier model is a labelled training example: the redacted input, the accepted output, and — most valuable of all — the user's edit, which is a free human preference signal that most teams have to pay for. After four to six weeks of real usage we have a distillation set drawn from our exact task distribution. Then we train a small model to take over the highest-volume routes. Frontier model as teacher, small model as student, the user's Send-or-Edit as the reward.

This answers "can we build the intermediary model?" with: yes — and it arrives as a byproduct of running the product rather than a prerequisite for building it. That ordering is exactly what sir asked for when he said the agent shouldn't be blocked on the model.

It asks only one discipline of us today, and it must be done from the first commit or the opportunity is lost:

  • S1 sits behind a stable interface, so swapping the model underneath touches nothing else.
  • Every S2 call is logged in distillation-ready form: redacted input, model output, user verdict, timestamp.
  • The router reads its stage assignments from configuration, so moving a task from S2 to S1 is a config change and not a deployment.

The data flow between the three tiers

Sir asked specifically how the agent, the intermediary model and the frontier model would communicate. The answer is that they never talk to each other — they all talk to the router, which is the only component that knows all three exist.

message → S0 rules → if decided, act.
Otherwise → router → S1 local model → returns an answer and a confidence score.
If confidence is high → act. If low, or the task is drafting → router → privacy gateway → redact → S2 frontier → un-redact → act.
Every hop writes to the passport log and the distillation log.

The important property: the router decides on confidence, not on category. If it escalated whole categories, S2 volume would be fixed by policy. Escalating on confidence means that as S1 improves, S2 volume falls automatically and costs go down without anyone changing a rule. The system gets cheaper as it gets older.

What about running it on the phone?

An honest limitation worth raising yourself before it's raised at you: an on-device model cannot run the ingestion path. Mail arrives while the phone is asleep, and mobile operating systems restrict background execution precisely to protect battery. So the agent has to be server-side regardless, and the realistic home for S1 in V1 is a modest CPU machine we control — 16GB of RAM is enough to start.

On-device becomes coherent later, as an opt-in local-only mode: the phone becomes the trust boundary, processing happens when the app is open, and the user trades immediacy for absolute privacy. That's a genuine future feature and a strong story. It is not a V1 constraint, and pretending otherwise would build the wrong system.

Scale and operations

Sir's worry — "four users show up and it falls over" — is addressed by the same architecture that solves cost. Because 95% of messages never reach a paid API, throughput is bounded by our own cheap components rather than by an expensive external one.

  • Where the first bottleneck lands: not inference. It will be provider rate limits (Gmail's per-user ceiling, and the per-project ceiling once we have thousands of users) and the database. Both are cheap to scale horizontally; both need monitoring from V1.
  • Watch renewal is the highest-risk operational task in the system, because failure is silent on both ends — mail arrives, we hear nothing, and no error is raised anywhere. Daily renewal cron plus a heartbeat that alarms if a user produces no notifications for N hours.
  • Per-user budget enforcement in the router: on exhaustion, degrade to S1 quality rather than fail or overspend.
  • Backpressure — the queue is per-user ordered, so one user with a 50,000-message backlog cannot starve everyone else.

Who is the first cohort — a UET internal deployment, or a public beta?

V0 alone is a genuinely demoable product in two weeks. If the timeline is tighter, we ship Reclaim and Radar properly rather than compressing all three into something half-finished.

What I'd research next, in priority order

Two checks, not one. Does the platform permit it? — §03 is the reference. Does it survive our own cost and privacy constraints? — §05 and §06 are the reference.
