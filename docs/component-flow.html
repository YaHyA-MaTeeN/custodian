Five things the picture is designed to make obvious

Mail leaves at six points. Sensitive at 3, bulk at 4, known templates at 5 with no model at all, deadlines at 7, no-reply-needed at 9. Only what's left reaches the paid model.

One coloured box out of fourteen costs money. Everything else is free libraries, our own rules, or a small model on our own CPU.

There is exactly one door out of the building — stage 10. If it isn't in that box, it never left.

The approval gate is our code, not the model's manners.

Every correction feeds back as training data. The dashed line on the left. The system gets cheaper as it gets older, because stage 8 keeps absorbing work from stage 11.

The honest caveat to carry with this

Machine-generated mail is ~95% of the volume but only ~27% of what people actually open and keep. So every optimisation above buys real cost savings — and the mail that matters is human-written, which still goes to the good model.

Budget model calls against attention, not volume. And the percentages on this diagram are estimates until measured on a real 1,000-message mailbox.

What is deliberately never stored

Email bodies. An email is read, acted on, and the text is dropped. What persists is the extracted facts — a date, an amount, two labels — not the message.

Embeddings of email bodies. This is a real trap and worth knowing: an embedding is not a safe substitute for text. Published work recovers 92% of short inputs word-for-word from their embeddings alone. So we store typed facts, and treat any embedding we do hold with the same care as raw text.

Anything from sensitive mail. Stage 3 stops it before anything opens it. There is nothing to store.

Attachments. Dropped at stage 2, except when an invoice PDF is read for its embedded data.

The answer to "where does my writing style come from"

It is not a model trained on you, and it is not your inbox. It is a handful of replies you wrote yourself, plus a short description of your habits, handed to the model as examples at the moment it writes.

That means: nothing of yours is baked into a model, deleting your account removes it completely, and the only email text that ever leaves is your own outgoing writing — never anybody else's incoming mail.

The number to have ready

In published testing, every one of 1,404 real email agents was successfully hijacked, most within about two attempts. The action that held up best was sending mail — but only because some models volunteer a confirmation, and the attack works precisely by telling them not to.

That is why our gate is not a model behaviour. It is a step in our own code that the model has no way to reach, argue with, or skip. Nothing sends without a human pressing something.

The one sentence that resolves it

The words tell you what the email says. Your history tells you what you'll do about it.

Those are different questions, so they get different machinery. Stage 8 reads every word carefully — that's its whole job. Stage 9 asks a question the words genuinely cannot answer: an identical message from your boss and from a stranger needs a reply in one case and not the other, and nothing in the text distinguishes them.

Measured on real company mail, each feature group scored on its own: your history with the sender 0.692, history with that exact pair 0.638, what the email was understood to be — a request, a commitment — 0.595, who they are 0.594, who else is on it 0.591, timing 0.540, and the raw words 0.511 against a chance baseline of 0.500. Everything combined: 0.721.

So the real distinction is not text versus history — it is raw text versus interpreted text. Stage 8 turns the words into meaning; stage 9 uses that meaning as one signal among several, alongside history, recipients and timing. The only thing stage 9 never uses is the literal word-soup, which is the one thing that genuinely cannot answer its question.

How spam rescue works — four checks, once a day

We do not detect spam. The provider already did that. We read the folder it put things in.

1 · Ask for the spam folder's list — headers only, never a body. in:spam on Gmail, the junkemail folder on Outlook, \Junk on IMAP.

2 · Do we know this sender? ali@company.com — 47 emails, 41 replies → known. winner@lottery.tk — never seen → skip. noreply@shop.com — 312 emails, none ever opened → skip. Two of three gone on a table lookup.

3 · Is it cryptographically genuine? Read the stamp our own provider applied on arrival — dkim=pass; spf=pass. Pass means it really is from that domain. Fail means the name was forged — leave it in Spam.

4 · Surface it. "An email from Ali went to Spam." They say yes, we move it back. Reversible.

Step 3 is not optional. Anyone can write "Ali" as the sender. Without it, a forged email that landed in Spam because it failed verification would be rescued by us — undoing the filter's correct decision, with our credibility attached.

Read the "costs money" column

Nine of the ten features are free to run. Only drafting replies uses the paid model, and reminders touch it briefly to choose between candidate dates.

That is the whole commercial argument: the features people ask for most — sorting, junk, reminders, knowing who owes you — are the ones that cost us nothing.

Stage 5 is two different things wearing one name

Half one — the answer arrived attached. A meeting invite carries a file with the exact time, place and attendees. Some invoice PDFs carry the full invoice as data inside them. A Wallet pass — the thing behind "add to Apple Wallet" — carries the flight number, gate, seat and barcode. We open the file and read the fields. Nothing is interpreted.

Half two — a shape we worked out ourselves. Your electricity bill looks identical every month; only the numbers change. After 5–10 of them we record where the amount and due date sit, and from then on we just look at those spots. No model for that sender again.

Why it earns its place: bills, bookings, orders and tickets are the most common mail people get — and the ones reminders are most useful for. The most valuable reminders come from the emails that cost us nothing to understand.

⚠️ What happens when a sender changes their layout

A big redesign is safe automatically. We match on the whole skeleton of the email, so a redesign no longer matches and the layout simply is not used. It falls through to the normal path.

A small change is the real risk — the skeleton still matches but a field has moved. Three things catch it: all fields or none (a missing field discards the whole extraction, not just that field); does the value look right (this sender is never PKR 12345); and a rebuild every 30 days, so anything that slipped through self-heals.

And the rule that makes the residual risk acceptable: a layout extraction never triggers an action. It only ever becomes something we show you — "K-Electric, PKR 8,450, due the 15th." We never pay, send, or delete on the strength of one. The worst outcome is a reminder with a wrong number that you read and correct.

And if it fails entirely? The email goes down the normal path. A broken layout costs us a fraction of a cent — never a wrong action.

The two hard constraints on this choice

Context window is not negotiable. The 22M model truncated at 256 word-pieces — most real emails are longer, so it would have silently dropped the bottom of every long message and never told us. ModernBERT takes 8,192. That alone decides it.

Fine-tuning is mandatory, not optional. Off-the-shelf models at these sizes failed under every measurement found. But the bar is low: 100–1,000 labelled examples, and more categories makes the crossover arrive sooner, not later. A 355M fine-tuned encoder beat 175B GPT-3 by 8.6 points with 50 labels per task.

Where these run, and what that costs

Both small models run on our own CPU through ONNX Runtime, which is ~14× faster than the naive path — a bigger win than quantisation.

Measured break-even against paying an API: self-hosting the classifier pays off at about 2.4% utilisation; self-hosting the writer needs about 24%. So we host the small ones and rent the big one. Small encoders are 25–100× cheaper and ~3,500× faster than an API call for this kind of work.

Why the day-one win matters commercially

Both things they see in the first half hour — the junk pile and the who-is-waiting list — run on envelopes alone. No model, no API call, no cost.

That is the demo. It works before a single paid call has been made, which means the product proves itself before it costs anything.

Verify this yourself — the numbers to check

Reading 40,000 envelopes means 40,000 metadata fetches. Gmail charges quota units per call and limits units per user per second, so the wall-clock time is quota-bound, not bandwidth-bound.

The 15–30 minute figure is calculated from published quota limits and should be confirmed against the live docs before it is quoted to a customer. Search: Gmail API usage limits and Microsoft Graph throttling limits. The equivalent Outlook figure will differ.

How to compare any two of these honestly

Published numbers are a shortlist, not a decision. A model that scores 98 on its own test set scored 0.27 on real documents in independent testing. That gap is the norm, not an outlier.

The only comparison that settles anything: take 200 emails from a real mailbox, label them by hand, and run both options. That is an afternoon, and it beats every leaderboard.

The three honest answers on scale

Is it fast? Everything except the writer finishes in well under a second. The writer takes 1–3 seconds and runs on about 5% of mail — and nobody is waiting on it, because it arrives as a message. Nothing in this system is on a user's critical path.

Does it scale? Every stage is stateless except the database. Scaling is copying the machine. There is no shared queue, no coordination, no leader. Two users never touch each other's work.

Where does it break first? Not our compute — the provider's rate limits. Google and Microsoft cap how fast we may read a mailbox. That is the real ceiling, it applies per user, and no amount of hardware changes it. Design for it: read in the background, never make a user wait on a fetch.

The capacity number — and how it is derived

Measured: a comparable encoder converted to ONNX reached 72 documents/second single-threaded and 233/second batched on a 16-core CPU. Our classifier is larger, so it will be slower.

Calculated, conservatively: even at 20 emails/second, one machine classifies ~1.7 million emails a day. A heavy user gets 200. So one $100 machine covers the classifier for thousands of users.

This is arithmetic on a measured figure, not a measurement of our own model. Verify it before quoting it as fact — but the margin is so large that even being wrong by 10× leaves the conclusion standing.

Why we do not buy a GPU

Measured break-even: self-hosting the classifier beats paying an API at about 2.4% utilisation — so hosting it is obviously right, and CPU is enough. Self-hosting the writer needs about 24% utilisation before it beats the API, and a hosted writer would need a GPU sitting idle most of the day.

So: host the small models on CPU, rent the big one by the token, own no GPU.

Why each step is built the way it is

Every stage, the alternative we rejected, and the reason. These are the questions to expect.

StageWhat we didThe obvious alternativeWhy we rejected it
1Connect to the mailboxBuild our own email appNobody switches email clients. Connecting means we work with what they already use, on day one, on every platform.
2Strip quoted history firstSend the whole emailMeasured 85% token reduction for one library and no accuracy cost. Skipping it means paying 6× more for identical results.
3Sensitive check runs firstCheck it along with everything elseIf it ran later, something would already have opened the email before we decided it shouldn't be opened. Order is the whole point.
4Header rules before any modelLet the model decide what is junkThe sender declares bulk mail in a header — required by law since 2024. Reading a declaration is free and exact; guessing costs money on 100% of mail and is sometimes wrong.
5Templates and standard formatsRead every bill with a model80–90% of transactional mail is templated. Once we know where the amount sits, extraction is free forever. A model would re-solve the same problem every month.
6Escalate on confidenceEscalate on categoryWith confidence, cost falls automatically as the models improve — no code change. With categories, cost is fixed forever.
7Rules find dates, code does mathsAsk the model for the deadlineEncoders score below random chance on date arithmetic. Rules reach ~90%. This is not a close call.
8149M, fine-tuned, on our CPUSmaller: 22M
Bigger: a 7B model		22M sits below a cliff — loses 8.7 points — and truncates at 256 tokens, silently dropping the end of long emails. 7B needs a GPU and buys ~1 point over 150M.
9Decision trees, no language modelUse the classifier for this tooRaw text predicts replying at 0.51 against a 0.50 coin flip. There is nothing for a language model to do. Interaction history alone reaches 0.69.
10Exactly one egress pointCall the API wherever convenientWith one door, we can prove what left. With several, we can only hope. This is the difference between a privacy claim and a privacy guess.

The honest bottleneck

The hard part is not compute, money, or model choice. It is getting the first few hundred labelled emails. Everything else is minutes and pennies.

That is also the good news: the crossover where a fine-tuned small model beats a prompted frontier model is 100–1,000 examples — and it arrives sooner when there are more categories, not later. This is a few days of work, not a research programme.
