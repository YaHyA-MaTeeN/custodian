The main deliverable

Built

A complete build specification

The diagram showed what the system does. It did not say how to build any of it. That gap is now closed — roughly 2,500 lines covering every stage, every library, every model.

  • All four ways to reach a mailbox — Gmail API, Microsoft Graph, JMAP, IMAP — what each one is, what it can and cannot do, and where each one breaks
  • Every stage of the pipeline, with each library named, what it does, and the specific trap it carries
  • Every model — where it comes from, what it costs to train, why that size and not another
  • Permissions, database design, deployment, and how we prove any of it works

Each section opens with a plain-English summary, so the document can be read two ways: skim the summaries to understand the system, read the detail when actually writing that part.

Two faults found before we built them

Corrected

Stage 4 was treating "machine-generated" as "junk"

The original design filtered bulk mail out early. That is wrong, and the reason is worth stating plainly: bank statements, flight bookings, invoices and password resets are all machine-generated. So is a shop's sale email — which is noise to most people and the entire reason someone opened their mailbox if they are waiting on a price drop.

Any category list we write is wrong for somebody. The fix removes categories entirely.

We do not decide what is important. We measure what this person actually engages with.

The day-one mailbox scan already tells us whether someone has ever opened a given sender. That is personal, learned, needs no taxonomy — and costs nothing, because it is the same data another stage already uses.

A rule we adopted as a result: a rule may route mail; it may never discard it. The user is the only thing that deletes.

Corrected

Stage 5 was misnamed, and its failure mode was undocumented

It was called "template matching", but three of the four things it does are not templates — they are reading a data file the sender already attached. Renamed, and the two halves separated.

More importantly, its real risk is now written down: when a sender changes their email layout slightly, the extraction can silently read the wrong field. Three defences were specified, and one rule makes the residual risk acceptable:

An extracted layout never triggers an action. It only ever becomes something we show the user.

The output is a reminder — "K-Electric, due the 15th" — which a person reads and can correct. We never pay, send or delete on the strength of one.

Added today

New

Spam rescue

Spam filters make mistakes in both directions, and the second kind is the one people actually complain about: a real email buried in the spam folder and never seen. A university offer with five days to reply. A letter from a court.

Once a day we read the spam folder's envelopes only — never a message — and surface anything from a sender the person genuinely corresponds with.

The safety condition is the whole design. Sender names are trivially forged, so we only surface a message whose cryptographic verification passed. If it failed, that is very likely why it was filtered in the first place, and we leave it there.

Costs nothing to run: no new permissions, no model, headers only. No commercial email product looks in the spam folder at all.

Decided

How the user talks to it, given text input rather than buttons

The instruction was text, not buttons. That makes every single interaction something the system has to interpret — so the design has to make interpretation easy rather than clever.

Our own code remembers what is on screen, so the model is only ever asked a multiple-choice question.

When someone types "make it shorter", the model never has to work out what "it" means — our code already knows. It is handed eight options and picks one. It cannot invent an action we did not offer, and when no draft is pending, "send" is not on the list at all.

Approval defaults to no. Only an unambiguous yes sends anything — which is safer than a button, because anything unclear does nothing.

One machine covers a hundred thousand users. No graphics card is needed at any size. The second server is for safety, not speed.

The finding worth carrying: at scale, servers are about one percent of the bill and the paid AI is the rest. That is precisely why the pipeline has fourteen stages — every email that exits early is money, and the hosting choice barely matters.

Where the numbers come from

Honesty

Every figure in the specification is marked as one of three kinds. This matters more than any individual number, because it is what makes the document safe to quote from.

Measured

Published results from other people's work — model size effects, the cost of prompt injection attacks, how much of an email is repeated text.

Calculated

Our arithmetic on those measurements — memory footprints, users per server, break-even points. Sound, but not our own measurements.

Not yet known

What share of a real mailbox exits at each stage, our true cost per user, how many users one server actually holds.

All three unknowns are answerable in about a week with one real mailbox. I would rather bring a measured number next week than a confident guess today.

Needing your decision

Open

Four questions, in the order they block work

QuestionWhy it matters now
Is WhatsApp still the channel? About 75% of people in the UK use it — but only around a quarter in the US and a third in Canada. Two of our three markets barely use it. Business registration for it also takes weeks, so this blocks work either way.
Gmail or Outlook first? These are business markets, which leans Microsoft. But Microsoft's terms restrict what mail data may be used for unless the customer explicitly permits more — worth an hour of legal reading before committing.
Which country first? Decides where we host, and therefore which provider we start on.
What do we charge? Products in this category priced under $50 per user keep only 23% of their revenue year to year. Above $250 they keep 70%. This decides more than the feature list does.

Next

Planned

The specification is complete enough to start building against. The most useful next step is not more writing — it is connecting one real mailbox and measuring the three unknowns above, which turns the estimates in this document into facts.

That also produces the first labelled training data, which is the genuine bottleneck of the whole project. Not compute, not money — a few hundred emails labelled by hand.
