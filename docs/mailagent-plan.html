Product & Implementation Research — v1 for review

Inbox Custodian

An email agent that works your inbox for you and asks permission in chat — so you stop opening your inbox. This document converts the brief into a scoped product, checks every feature against what Gmail, Outlook and WhatsApp actually permit, and sets out an architecture that runs at roughly $1–3 per user per month without shipping anyone's mail to a frontier model in the clear.

01

The sharpened idea

The brief was an abstract: an agent that reads your mail, learns your tone, drafts replies, asks you on WhatsApp, sorts your junk, and warns you about deadlines. Those are features. This is the product they add up to.

Positioning

Every email AI on the market — Gmail's Smart Reply, Superhuman, Shortwave — puts the intelligence inside the mail client, so you still have to go there. We invert it. The agent lives in the channel you already check all day, and the inbox becomes a system of record you visit once a week. The approval loop is the product; the inbox work is the plumbing.

That inversion gives us three things nothing else in the category has:

  1. Zero new surface to learn. The first version has no app to install. A message arrives, you get a chat asking "here's my reply — send, edit, or ignore?", and you answer in one word.
  2. It writes in your words, not AI words. Tone is not a setting the user picks from a dropdown. We mine the user's own Sent folder and retrieve their closest past replies as style anchors, so the draft already sounds like them on day one and improves every time they edit one.
  3. It can prove what it did and did not read. Every processed message carries a record of exactly what left our servers, in what redacted form, to which model. Trust is the hardest thing to sell in this category, and it is the one thing a competitor cannot copy with a prompt.

One-line pitch for a user: "It cleans out your inbox, tells you what actually matters, drafts the replies in your voice, and pings you to approve them — you never have to open Gmail."

The finding that matters

The three highest-value jobs — the vault, the cleanup and the scope control — need no language model at all. They run on message headers and deterministic rules. 03

Feasibility ledger

"Attach to the user's mailbox" cannot mean "attach to two vendors." So the first question is how far across the email world we can actually reach — and the honest answer is that providers differ so much in what they permit that feature parity across them is impossible. That single fact shapes the connector architecture.

The provider landscape

Every mailbox a user might bring falls into one of four tiers, and the tier determines what the product can do for them:

TierProvidersAccess routeWhat we get
T1 — Full API
Everything works		 Gmail & Google Workspace, Outlook.com & Microsoft 365 Gmail API, Microsoft Graph — REST + OAuth Real webhooks, labels & categories, server-side search, threading, calendar. 100% of the feature set.
T2 — API, smaller
Nearly everything		 Zoho Mail, Fastmail Zoho REST API; Fastmail via JMAP JMAP is genuinely excellent — JSON over HTTPS with real push, one round trip, proper state sync. Better than IMAP in every way. Adoption outside Fastmail is still thin, so it's a bonus, not a strategy.
T3 — OAuth IMAP

T4 — Out of reach

Architectural consequence

Because tiers differ this much, a connector cannot just be "a mailbox." Every connector declares a capability set — push, labels, categories, threads, server_search, calendar — and the product degrades gracefully against it. A Yahoo user gets folders instead of tabs and a 2-minute delay instead of instant, and the UI says so rather than silently doing less. Design this on day one; retrofitting it after building Gmail-first is a rewrite.

Three decisions that unblock the whole project

1. We do not need CASA to build, demo, or pilot. Google's OAuth "Testing" mode allows up to 100 test users on restricted scopes with no verification at all. That covers the entire MVP, the demo, and a real pilot cohort. Separately, a Google Workspace administrator can install an app internally for their own domain without public verification — which means the university's own @uet.edu.pk domain is the ideal first deployment. Sir's own example of wanting it on just the ORIC mailbox is, by coincidence, exactly the deployment that dodges the biggest obstacle. We budget for CASA only when we go public.

2. Build the notification layer channel-agnostic, and ship Telegram first. A Telegram bot needs no business verification, costs nothing, and is about a day's work — so the demo is never hostage to Meta's approval queue. Behind one NotificationChannel interface, WhatsApp becomes a swap, not a rewrite. And when we do turn WhatsApp on, the cost design is: send one utility template to open the conversation ("4 messages need you today — reply to start"), then run the entire day's approvals free-form inside the 24-hour window it opens. That turns an unbounded per-message cost into roughly one template per user per day.

3. Build Tier 1 natively; do not buy a unified email API. The managed aggregators — Aurinko from about $1 per connected account per month, Nylas at $15/month for 5 accounts then $2 each, Unipile around €5 — solve provider breadth for us. But look at what that does to the unit economics: at $1–3 per user per month in model costs, a $1–2 per-account sync fee roughly doubles our cost of goods, to buy access to providers that are a small minority of our users. The Gmail API and Microsoft Graph are free, well documented, and cover the overwhelming majority of the target market — including Pakistani universities, which run on Workspace for Education and Microsoft 365 EDU. So: build T1 natively, add one generic IMAP/JMAP connector for the tail (self-hosted, flat-cost — the EmailEngine model at a fixed annual fee is the shape to copy if we don't write our own), and revisit an aggregator only if the tail turns out to be where the paying users are.

The cost architecture

The brief's central constraint: do not send everything to a frontier model, or the product cannot be sold. Here is the number that constraint is worth, and the design that captures it.

The principle

The biggest cost lever is not which model we pick. It is not calling a model at all. Most of what an inbox needs is decidable from message headers, and most of the rest is decidable by a small model on our own hardware. The frontier model is reserved for the one job where quality is the entire product: writing the reply.

The three-stage cascade

S0
Header rules — no model, no body read
Sender reputation, List-Unsubscribe, Precedence: bulk, SPF/DKIM result, thread depth, and the strongest single signal in email: has this user ever replied to this sender? Sorts bulk, marketing and notifications, and flags the sensitive vault.
~70% of volume
$0.00
S1

Category, urgency tag, deadline and amount extraction, entity spotting, short thread summaries, and the redaction pass. Runs quantized on CPU. Escalates to S2 only when its own confidence is low.
~25% of volume
amortized
S2
Frontier model — redacted, compressed, cached
Reply drafting and genuinely ambiguous triage only. Reaches the provider with identities tokenized, quoted history stripped, and the thread compressed to the last message plus a local summary.
~5% of volume
~8 calls/day

05

The privacy angle

The brief asked for a smart angle — something that makes the system oblivious to user data without crippling it. This is it, and it is the same design as the cost architecture. That is not a coincidence: every byte we don't send is a byte we don't pay for and don't expose.

Mechanism 01
Sensitive Vault
OTPs, banking, health and legal mail are identified from headers alone at S0 — sender domain lists, subject patterns, and the short-body-with-a-numeric-code shape. Their bodies never enter any model, local or remote. The user gets "6-digit code from HBL — open in Gmail" and nothing more. Default on, user-editable.
Mechanism 02
Minimization by stage
S0 sees headers only. S1 sees bodies but runs entirely on our own hardware. S2 sees only what survives redaction. Each stage is given the least data that can do its job, and the boundary is enforced in code, not policy.
Mechanism 03
Tokenized egress
Before anything reaches a frontier model, a local NER pass swaps names, addresses, emails, phone numbers, account numbers and amounts for stable placeholders — <PERSON_1>, <ORG_2>, <AMOUNT_1>. The provider sees the skeleton of a conversation, never an identity. The draft is de-tokenized locally before the user ever reads it.
Mechanism 04
Data Passport
Every handled message carries a tappable receipt: what was read, what left, in what redacted form, to which model, at what cost, under what retention. It is a genuine user-facing feature, and it doubles as our evidence file for CASA and for Google's no-training commitment.
The storage rule

We store derived structure, never content. Message IDs, labels, extracted entities, style vectors, sender graph — yes. Raw bodies — no, beyond a short processing window. If our database is ever breached, the attacker gets embeddings and metadata, not anybody's mail. That sentence is the strongest claim in the entire pitch, and it is only true if we design for it from the first commit rather than bolting it on.

Two supporting commitments: we use a zero-retention, no-training API tier and say so plainly in the privacy policy — Google's Workspace policy requires it of us anyway — and the very first run is dry-run only. The agent shows the user exactly what it would have done to 1,900 messages and changes nothing until they say go. Nothing builds trust faster than a system that asks before its first move.

07

System design

Design principle

A deterministic pipeline with a model only at the leaves — not a free-roaming agent looping over tools on someone's mailbox. An agent that can call delete in a loop is a liability. A pipeline that proposes typed actions and executes them behind an approval gate is a product. The agentic part is the router and the approval loop, and its tool surface is deliberately tiny: label, archive, unsubscribe, draft_reply, schedule_reminder, ask_user. There is no delete — which is also what keeps us out of Google's Tier 3 assessment.

01
Connector layer
Gmail, Graph and generic IMAP/JMAP adapters normalizing to one Message envelope. Each declares a capability set so the product degrades gracefully per provider. Includes the watch-renewal daemon and a silence alarm.
02
Event queue
Per-user ordered, idempotent. Reprocessing the same message must never produce a second action.
03
S0 rules engine
Header-only classification and the vault check. Milliseconds, free, auditable.
04
S1 inference service
The local small model behind one stable interface, so it can be swapped or upgraded without touching callers.
05
Model router
Picks a stage from task type, S1 confidence, and the user's remaining monthly budget. Degrades to S1 rather than failing.
06
Privacy gateway
The only path to any external model. Redact, compress, call, de-redact, write the passport entry.
07
Profile store
Style vectors, sender graph, extracted entities, the rule store. Structured records — no message bodies.
08
Action executor
Every action idempotent, audited and undoable. Archive to a reviewable label; never delete.
09
Approval service
Channel adapters for Telegram, WhatsApp, push and web. One interface, so the channel is a config choice.

On RAG and vector databases

A vector index over the whole mailbox is the wrong tool for this problem, and it is worth being explicit about why: it is the most expensive option, it requires storing exactly the content we just promised not to store, and it solves a problem email doesn't have — email tasks are thread-scoped, not corpus-scoped. Three narrow retrievals do the job instead:

  • Thread-local context — already in hand, free, and covers the large majority of drafting needs.
  • Sender memory — one small structured record per correspondent: relationship, usual formality, last few subjects, typical response time.
  • Style exemplars — a small local vector index over the user's own Sent mail only, retrieving three to five past replies most similar to the incoming message, used as few-shot anchors in the draft prompt.

That third one is worth dwelling on, because it is how we deliver "learn his tone" without fine-tuning anything: retrieval instead of training means it is per-user, instant, needs no GPU, costs nothing to update, and improves every single time the user edits a draft. Embeddings are computed locally; we store the vectors, not the mail.

Natural-language settings, implemented safely

The brief was firm that we must not build Gmail's settings screen. The design: settings live as a structured rule store (trigger, condition, action, timing, scope). The model's only job is to translate a sentence into a rule diff — never to make decisions at runtime. The user sees the diff in plain English ("I'll change 4 appointment reminders from 2 days to 2 hours — ok?") and confirms. Cheap enough for S1, auditable, reversible, and the runtime stays fully deterministic.

08

Clients & platforms

The brief asked whether this needs an app, a Chrome extension, a Windows client, a Mac client, or an SDK. The answer is that the question mostly dissolves once the chat channel exists.

The rule

One backend, thin clients. Mail arrives while the phone is asleep, so the agent has to be server-side regardless — there is no version of this where the work happens on the handset. Once that is true, the chat channel becomes a zero-install client that covers Android, iOS, Windows, Mac and web on day one, for free.

SurfaceWhenReasoning
Chat channelTelegram → WhatsAppV0Every platform at once, nothing to install, and it is where the approval loop naturally lives. Build this and "which OS?" stops being a question.
Web dashboardV1Onboarding, connection management, the Data Passport ledger, and the once-a-month deep settings. Small.
Mobile appOne cross-platform codebaseV3Needed for real push notifications, a richer approval UI, and eventually a local-first mode. One codebase, not two.
Chrome extensionDeferredOnce approvals happen in chat, an in-Gmail overlay adds little and costs a whole maintained surface. Revisit only if users ask.
Public SDKNot nowThat is a platform play. We do not have a platform yet, and shipping one early would freeze internal interfaces we still need to change weekly.

One tension worth naming honestly rather than discovering later: an on-device model cannot run the ingestion path. Background execution limits and battery mean a phone cannot reliably process mail as it arrives. The realistic home for our small model in V1 is a cheap CPU box we control. On-device becomes coherent later, as an opt-in "local-only mode" where the phone is the trust boundary and the user accepts that processing happens when the app is open. That is a real future feature and a strong privacy story — but it is a V3 conversation, not a V1 constraint.

09

The small-model track

Surveyed as directed, and deliberately kept off the critical path — the agent is designed so that whatever sits underneath it can be replaced without the agent changing.

On the 10–100M parameter target: at that size you do not get a usable generative model. But we don't need one there, because the jobs we actually route to S1 split into two kinds, and the small kind fits that range precisely:

JobRight sizeCandidateRuns on
Category, urgency, is-this-bulk30–110MEncoder + classification head (MiniLM / DistilBERT class), fine-tuned on our own labeled trafficCPU, milliseconds
PII detection for redaction~100MSmall NER modelCPU
Style-exemplar retrieval~100–300MSentence embedding modelCPU
Short summaries, rule diffs, extraction0.5–1.7BQwen3-0.6B, Gemma 3 (270M–1B), Llama 3.2 1B, SmolLM2 — quantized, via llama.cpp or ONNX RuntimeCPU, or a modest GPU
Sequencing — the important part

Don't train a model first. Collect the data first. Every S2 call we make is a labeled training example: the redacted input, the accepted output, and — most valuable of all — the user's edit, which is a free human preference signal. After four to six weeks of real usage we have a distillation set drawn from our exact task distribution, and then we fine-tune a small model to take over the highest-volume routes. Frontier model as teacher, small model as student, the user's Send-or-Edit as the reward.

So the answer to "can we build the intermediary model" is yes — but it arrives as a byproduct of running the product, not as a prerequisite for building it. That ordering is what keeps the agent unblocked, which is exactly the constraint given. The only thing it asks of us today is one architectural discipline: S1 sits behind a stable interface from the first commit, and every S2 call is logged in distillation-ready form (redacted input, output, user verdict) from day one. Skip that and the training set doesn't exist when we want it.

One caveat to carry into that work: Google's Workspace policy forbids training generalized models on Workspace API data. Distillation must therefore be scoped as personalization — per-user or per-tenant adapters — or trained on synthetic and consented data only. Worth resolving with counsel before the first fine-tune, not after.

Explicitly not building

Naming these protects the timeline, and each one was a live temptation in the brief:

  • No SMTP/IMAP server and no email client — we attach to Gmail and Outlook and stay inside their boundaries.
  • No auto-send without approval, at any confidence level, in any release covered here.
  • No attachment or document processing in V1 — a large, separate problem with its own privacy surface.
  • No multi-account, no shared/team mailboxes, no CRM features, no Chrome extension before V3.
  • No mailbox-wide vector database, for the reasons in §07.
  • No Proton Mail or iCloud support, ever — and for a good reason. Proton is end-to-end encrypted, so its servers never hold plaintext and a server-side agent is architecturally impossible, not merely unsupported. iCloud offers no Mail API and would require asking users for an app-specific password, which is exactly the trust posture we're selling against. Both are principled exclusions, and worth saying out loud rather than leaving as gaps.
  • No IMAP IDLE connection farm — Tier 3 mailboxes are polled on a schedule.
11

Decisions needed

Five questions where the answer changes what gets built. Everything else in this document I can proceed on.

01

Who is the first cohort — a UET internal deployment, or a public consumer beta?

Recommendation: UET internal. A Workspace admin can install it for the domain with no public verification, which sidesteps the single biggest obstacle in §03, and gives us real users with real mailboxes in week three instead of month four.

02

Is Telegram acceptable as the demo channel, with WhatsApp in V2?

Recommendation: yes. It removes a hard external dependency from the critical path for roughly a day of work, and the channel abstraction means switching later costs nothing.

03

Do we have a machine for the S1 local model?

A CPU box with 16GB of RAM is enough to start; a modest GPU helps but is not required. This determines whether V1 slips.

04

What is the acceptable cost ceiling per user per month?

This single number sets the routing policy. $2 gets us Sonnet-quality drafts throughout; $1 means Haiku for most drafts with escalation only on the hardest. Both are viable — but the router needs the number.

05
